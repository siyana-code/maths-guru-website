# Who Are We Actually Teaching?

**Research date: 9 October 2026**

A research document is useless if it does not end with a clear picture of a real
person. This is ours.

---

## The honest answer: we have several students, not one

There is no single Sri Lankan maths student. Trying to design for an average
person will produce something that fits nobody.

We work with **four real segments**, and we should be honest about which one we
are building for first.

---

## Segment 1: The Grade 6 student who has quietly fallen behind

**This is our first target. Here is why.**

### Who they are

- Age 11-12. Grade 6 is the first year of secondary school.
- Bright enough. School reports and parents say "she is clever".
- Failing maths, or barely passing.
- Cannot tell you what is wrong, because nobody has ever explained it to them.
- Sitting in a class of 40-50 where the teacher moves at the speed of the
  middle, and they have been lost since the gap first opened.

### How the gap opens

It almost never opens because of one big mistake. It opens like this:

- Grade 6 introduces **fractions**
- Fractions depend on **LCM and HCF**, which depend on **prime factorisation**
- A child who never really got **multiplication tables** cannot do any of it
- So they memorise the steps without understanding
- They pass the immediate test, then hit the next topic and fall further
- By Grade 8 they have a maths identity: "I am bad at maths"

**Nobody told them they were bad at maths. They worked it out themselves from
years of evidence.**

### What they need

- Someone to go back to the thing they actually missed, not the thing that is
  supposed to be hard now
- To feel stupid-proof while it happens
- Small wins, early and often

### What they do not need

- A motivational poster
- Harder problems
- Being told they are "not trying hard enough"
- Another explanation in the same style that did not work the first time

### Why this segment first

They have the most to gain, they are furthest behind, and **nobody is competing
for them.** The competitive segments (O/L, A/L, Tamil medium) all have
businesses already.

## Segment 2: The Grade 11 O-Level student and the panic

### Who they are

- Age 16-17. The year everything decides.
- Wants a specific grade, not just a pass. Distinctions and A passes come with
  a limited number of university places.
- Past papers are their Bible. They do them, they get marks, they do not
  understand why the marks are low.
- Studying harder and getting the same result.

### The core problem: knowing is not doing

They can watch a full lesson and understand every line. Then in the exam:

- Run out of time because the arithmetic was slow
- Freeze, and their mind goes blank
- Remember the formula but not which situation needs it
- Make a carry error in an easy sum and lose the thread of the whole question

**This is maths anxiety working exactly as the research describes.** It eats
working memory, and it hits hardest under time pressure.

### What they need

- Fluency in the boring arithmetic so it stops eating working memory
- Practice identifying *which method applies*, not just executing one
- Past papers with detailed marking schemes, not just answers
- Reassurance that slow is fine and speed comes later

### The wrong instinct to fight

Students in this group tend to do more past papers. More practice on the same
blocked topic is the least useful thing they can do.

## Segment 3: The Tamil-medium student

**The most neglected segment in Sri Lankan maths, and possibly the biggest
opportunity.**

### Why they are neglected

Out of roughly 1,066 indexed O/L maths papers online:

- **643 Sinhala**
- **412 English**
- **10 Tamil**

Against a school system where about a quarter of schools are Tamil-medium.

**That is 1% of content for 25% of the students.**

### Why it is not just a translation job

Because of a policy fact that surprises people:

> **Mathematics is the one subject the Sri Lankan government has actively
> pushed to teach in English from Grade 6 onwards.**

So a Tamil-medium student may be *learning* maths in English while *thinking* in
Tamil. They can follow the symbols but cannot ask a question in the language
they think in.

### The consequence for them

Per World Bank data, only about **10%** of Sri Lankan students reach target
English proficiency. So many reach the maths, understand the diagrams, and then
get stuck on one word in the question and cannot get help.

**You cannot ask a question you cannot phrase.**

### What they need

- Tamil as the language of *thinking*
- English as the language of the *symbols*
- Both, side by side

### The honest business warning

This is the bigger mission and the smaller revenue. 25% of the market, less
money per family, and Tamil-medium parents mostly want Tamil-medium *tuition*,
which is a human relationship we cannot replace.

> **Treat Tamil as a mission decision, not a revenue one.**

## Segment 4: The anxious parent

**Not a student. But the one who pays, and the one we currently ignore.**

### Who they are

The mother or father who saw a 40% on a term test and is now
researching-at-11pm.

- They did the same maths, thirty years ago, and remember being anxious
- They cannot help with the homework any more
- Tuition costs LKR 2,000-3,000 a month and they are not sure it is working
- They see the child struggling and do not know what to do

### What they actually want

**Evidence.** Not encouragement. Evidence.

They will never see the improvement themselves, because the child will not
volunteer it. They need to be told:

- "Your child can now do this. Three months ago they could not."
- "They got a wrong answer here, and worked out why by themselves."
- "They practised on 6 days out of the last 7."

That last one is not vanity. It tells a parent their child has a **habit**, and
a habit is the thing that survives a difficult month.

### The one statistic that matters to us

Our competitor research turned up this: **students do not know what improves
learning**, they systematically undervalue it, and they cannot predict their own
test performance.

Which means:

> **A parent reporting on a child's practice habit is doing something the
> student genuinely cannot do for themselves. It is not nagging. It is
> information.**

## The relationship between them

This is the important part.

```
        PARENT pays and decides
             |
        STUDENT uses and quits
             |
        both want: "my child is getting better at maths"
        they disagree about what that looks like
```

- Student wants it easy and quick. Parent wants it proven.
- Student feels the pain. Parent sees the report card.
- If only the student is happy, no money comes.
- If only the parent is happy, no usage.

> **The product has to serve both or it serves neither.**

| | Student | Parent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cares about | Is this hard? Do I understand? | Is this working? |
| Fears | Looking stupid | Wasting money and time |
| Needs | Instant progress, dignity | Evidence, honesty |
| Leaves when | Frustrated | Disappointed |

## Constraints that apply to all of them

These are not opinions. They are the numbers.

- **Mobile first.** 80% of device use is smartphones. Only 21% of households own
  a computer, and rural households 18%.
- **Low data.** Students spend over LKR 2,000 a month on data for school and
  tuition. Text and images beat video.
- **Power cuts happen.** A nationwide blackout in February 2025. Any session
  must survive losing power.
- **Phone sharing.** Likely a family phone, not a personal one. Assume
  interruption.
- **Sinhala and Tamil are not optional.** Sinhala Medium is the largest group,
  Tamil Medium about a quarter of schools, English Medium only about 1.4% of
  students.
- **"Grade 6" is the real name.** Sri Lanka calls it Grade 6. "Year 7" is a UK
  mapping we should avoid.

## What we are building for, stated plainly

**Primary: the Grade 6 student who has fallen behind without being told.**

Because:

- They have the most to gain
- Nobody is competing for them
- The content is simpler and cheaper for us to build
- Fixing the foundation has the longest payoff
- If we get this right, the O-Level student largely follows for free

**Secondary: the O-Level student and their anxious parent.** Same engine, more
questions, past papers.

**Long-term: Tamil medium.** The most neglected group in the country.

---

## What we still need to learn

We are guessing on all of this. We have no interviews, no surveys, no student
in the room.

**The single most valuable thing we could do next is talk to ten students and
ten parents.** Half an hour each. Specifically:

- Which chapter do they hate, and what do they think went wrong?
- What do they already use, and what did they give up on and why?
- What did they pay for tuition, and did it work?
- What would make them open this on a phone with one bar of signal?

> **Everything in this folder is a desk assumption until we do that.** That is
> the first item in [the roadmap](./13-roadmap.md), and it is not optional.
