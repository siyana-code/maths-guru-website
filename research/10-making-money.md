# Can This Make Money?

**Research date: 9 October 2026**

An honest look at whether this can pay for itself. The short answer is: not
soon, and probably not the way we first assume.

---

## The starting position

Being realistic about what we have:

- A part-time teaching idea, alongside an internship
- A free research corpus
- No revenue, no users, no product
- Competition that includes businesses with thousands of students

**The honest answer is that MathsGuru should be free, and probably should stay
free.** There is a good argument for that beyond generosity, and we should make
it deliberately rather than drifting into it.

---

## Why free is the correct strategy

### 1. The market price is LKR 1,500-3,000/month

That is what a Sri Lankan family pays for tuition. Out of a household education
budget that averages about LKR 2,400 a month.

> **We are competing for a genuinely small amount of money. A family choosing
> between LKR 2,000 of tuition and LKR 2,000 of something else is making a real
> choice, and we are usually the "something else".**

### 2. Our competitors are already cheaper than we could be

| Service | Price |
| --- | --- |
| Online maths classes | LKR 1,500-2,000/month |
| SriAI (AI tutor) | LKR 1,000/month |
| Tuition | LKR 1,500-3,000/month |
| Free YouTube | LKR 0 |

**We cannot win a price fight at the bottom.** And we should not try. The
Tamil-medium student we most want to reach has the least money, and charging
them is exactly backwards.

### 3. Charging removes the thing that makes us useful

Our entire value proposition is being free where tuition is not. The moment we
charge, we are another LKR 1,500 subscription competing with LKR 1,500 of
actual human teaching.

### 4. Free is how the good platforms do it

Khan Academy, Siyavula, Alcumus, GeoGebra and Past Papers are all free. The
pattern of the sector is not a coincidence.

## The realistic funding paths

If we want this to sustain itself, these are the options in rough order of
reality.

### Option 1: A teacher gets paid. We do not.

**Most likely, and worth saying plainly.**

We teach part-time. That is the income. The website is a tool that makes our
teaching better and lets us reach more students than one room allows.

This is a completely legitimate outcome. Many good educational projects work
exactly this way.

The risk: if we build the website and it does not help us get or keep students,
we have spent months for nothing. **So the website needs to demonstrably improve
our own teaching first.**

### Option 2: Donations from parents

Guthenberg, and free Sri Lankan education projects, run on small donations.

- Very low expectations. LKR 5,000 from one parent who is delighted is a win.
- Costs nothing to set up.
- Awkward to ask for.
- Worth adding a button and seeing what happens, rather than planning around it.

### Option 3: Grants and funding

Realistic sources for education projects:

- International education foundations
- Sri Lankan CSR programmes, often run by banks and large firms
- Innovation and startup programmes

**Pros:** real money, often 6-12 months at a time.

**Cons:** you write a lot of applications, and the report writing is real work.
Many grants require matching funds or a registered organisation.

**Only pursue this after the product works.** Applying for funding to build
something speculative wastes both time.

### Option 4: Sponsored content, done carefully

Something like Khan Academy's model, where an education sponsor funds free
content for students.

- Keeps content free for students
- Needs care: sponsor maths content, and trust is gone

**This is possible and we should keep it in mind. It should never influence what
we teach or how we explain it.**

### Option 5: A freemium tier, later and small

The platforms that do this charge parents, not students, and keep the student
experience free.

If it ever happens, the only defensible paid version is **parent-facing
convenience**, not better teaching:

- Printable worksheets and offline packs
- Parent reports with history
- Multiple children under one account

> **Never paywall the actual teaching.** If a family can only afford part of it,
> they get none of it, and we have lost the student who needed us most.

### Option 6: Sell to other teachers or tutors

A real possibility: if we build excellent diagnostic practice, other teachers
may want it for their students.

- Higher prices are realistic, in a different market
- Small numbers, but good money per customer
- Requires features teachers actually want: assign, track, printable, reports
- Needs teacher discovery, which is a whole marketing problem of its own

Worth keeping in mind once teachers are using it anyway.

---

## What we should not do

| Idea | Why not |
| --- | --- |
| **Sell paid lessons** | Competes directly with tutors who are better at it and cheaper in trust |
| **Charge students** | The students who need us least can pay. The ones who need us most cannot. |
| **Ads** | Maths sites with ads are exactly the experience we are trying to replace |
| **Sell data** | The student data we would hold is the most sensitive we could hold. Children. Exam anxiety. Do not. |
| **A marketplace** | Every directory in Sri Lanka has tried this. Revenue exists but inventory is thin and it takes years. |
| **Take a cut of tuition payments** | The payment step is manual and broken everywhere. Automating someone else's broken payment flow is not a product. |

---

## The costs we should expect

Realistically close to zero for the first year:

| Item | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Hosting | Free (Cloudflare Pages) |
| Domain | ~LKR 1,500/year |
| Content and tools | Free and open source |
| Our time | **The actual cost, and it is large** |

**The only significant cost is our time**, and the only thing we genuinely cannot
scale is the writing of clear explanations in three languages.

---

## The strategic case for free

We should stop treating free as a temporary state and treat it as the product.

1. **It reaches the students who need us most**, not the ones who can pay
2. **It makes us a tool rather than a competitor**, so teachers share us instead
   of blocking us
3. **It removes the biggest conversion barrier** - a student does not have to
   ask a parent for money to practise fractions
4. **It maximises the SEO and sharing benefit.** Free content gets linked,
   searched and recommended. Paid content gets locked inside a login.
5. **It is defensible in a way a feature list is not.** Anyone can copy a
   lesson. Nobody can copy being free in a market where tuition is not.

---

## The honest financial summary

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can this pay for bills? | **No, not any time soon** |
| Should it cost students money? | **No** |
| How does it become worth the time? | It improves our teaching, reaches more students, and builds something durable |
| When might money arrive? | Grants or sponsorship, after it demonstrably works, never as a precondition |
| What is the real resource we spend? | **Our time, which is why the roadmap must be small** |

---

## The rule we should adopt

> **Never charge a student for the teaching. Ever. Not even once, "just to
> test".**
>
> Charging changes what the product is. It turns a free tool that helps
> everyone into a business that helps whoever pays, and it puts us directly in
> competition with the human tutors who are better at this than we are.
>
> If the website is going to make money, it should be from something other than
> the students using it.
