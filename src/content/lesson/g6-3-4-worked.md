## What this is about

Adding fractions is easy **when the bottom numbers match**:

$$
\frac{1}{4} + \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{4}
$$

Add the tops, keep the bottom. That works because every part is the same size.

**The hard part is when the bottom numbers do not match.**

$$
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \ ?
$$

You cannot add the tops here. You cannot just keep a bottom either. Something
has to change first.

> That something is **equivalent fractions**. This is why competency 3.2 exists.
> If you skipped it, go back to [3.2](/grade/6/3-2) first. Everything on this
> page depends on it.

---

## The one rule

> **To add or subtract fractions, the bottom numbers must be the same.**

If they are not, you must **rewrite one of them** as an equivalent fraction
until they match.

That is the whole method. Everything else is detail.

---

## Why you cannot add across different bottoms

$$
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}
$$

Here is the problem. In $\frac{1}{2}$ you split the whole into **2** pieces and
took 1. In $\frac{1}{3}$ you split it into **3** pieces and took 1.

Those pieces are **different sizes**. You would be adding a "half" piece to a
"third" piece, and they cannot be combined without a common unit.

> **Think of it like money.** 50 rupees + 30 rupees is easy because both are
> rupees. But 50 rupees + 30 cents needs converting first. The bottom number is
> the currency. Two different currencies cannot be added directly.

---

## Worked example: same bottoms

### The problem

> Calculate $\frac{3}{7} + \frac{2}{7}$

### Step 1 — Check the bottom numbers

Left bottom: **7**. Right bottom: **7**.

**Why this step matters:** checking first saves you doing the whole problem
twice. If they already match, the easy method applies. If they do not, you need
the hard method. Ninety per cent of students skip this check and then get
confused about which method they are using.

### Step 2 — Add the top numbers only

$$
3 + 2 = 5
$$

**Why:** each piece is one seventh. So 3 sevenths plus 2 sevenths is 5 of the
same size. It is genuinely just counting pieces.

### Step 3 — Keep the bottom number

$$
\frac{3 + 2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}
$$

**Why:** the size of each piece has not changed. Nothing was resized, so the
bottom stays 7.

### Step 4 — Check

Can you add it up more than once? $\frac{7}{7} = 1$, so $\frac{5}{7}$ must be
less than 1. It is. Correct.

---

## Worked example: different bottoms

### The problem

> Calculate $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$

### Step 1 — Check the bottom numbers

Left bottom: **2**. Right bottom: **3**.

**They do not match.** So the easy method does not apply. We must convert.

### Step 2 — Find a bottom number that both can become

We need a number that 2 divides into exactly, and 3 divides into exactly.

- Multiples of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12
- Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12

The first one they **both** have is **6**. The next shared number is 12, and 6
is smaller, so 6 is the better choice.

> **Why find the smallest?** It keeps the numbers small and easy to work with.
> 6/12 works too, but you end up with bigger numbers and more chance of an
> arithmetic slip.

### Step 3 — Rewrite BOTH fractions with the bottom 6

For $\frac{1}{2}$: to turn 2 into 6, multiply by **3**. So the top goes 1 → 3.

$$
\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 \times 3}{2 \times 3} = \frac{3}{6}
$$

For $\frac{1}{3}$: to turn 3 into 6, multiply by **2**. So the top goes 1 → 2.

$$
\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \frac{2}{6}
$$

**Why both, and why different multipliers:** this is the step students get
wrong most. Each fraction has its **own** multiplier, worked out from **its own**
bottom number. The first fraction needs 2 to become 6, so it multiplies by 3. The
second needs 3 to become 6, so it multiplies by 2. Different multipliers for
different fractions, and that is correct.

### Step 4 — Now add normally

$$
\frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}
$$

**Why:** both bottoms are 6 now. We are adding sixths to sixths, which is just
counting pieces of the same size.

### Step 5 — Check

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = 0.5 + 0.333... = 0.833...$$

And $\frac{5}{6} = 0.833...$ Correct.

---

## Subtracting is exactly the same

Subtract the tops, keep the bottom, once they match.

$$
\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}
$$

You may simplify at the end if the answer can be reduced.

> **Careful:** $\frac{4}{6}$ and $\frac{2}{3}$ are both correct. Only the second
> is in its smallest form. Examiners usually want the smallest form, so simplify
> before writing the final answer.

---

## The mistake to avoid

This is the most common error in this topic.

> ## ❌ Never add the tops AND the bottoms.

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} \ne \frac{2}{5}$$

$$
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = 0.833... \qquad \frac{2}{5} = 0.4
$$

Adding both numbers like that gives a completely different answer. Addition
does **not** work that way for fractions.

The same applies to subtraction:

> ## ❌ $\frac{5}{7} - \frac{2}{7} \ne \frac{3}{0}$

The bottoms never add or subtract. They stay as they are, once they match.

---

## Another mistake: not simplifying

$$
\frac{2}{6} \text{ is correct, but } \frac{1}{3} \text{ is better}
$$

Both are worth 0.333. The official answer is normally written in its smallest
form. Check whether you can divide both numbers by the same amount before you
write the answer down.

---

## A useful shortcut for the exam

To add $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$:

1. Find the shared bottom (usually the **lowest common multiple** of b and d)
2. Multiply top and bottom of each fraction to reach it
3. Add the tops
4. Simplify

For $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6}$: the shared bottom is **12**.

$$
\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12} \qquad \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{12}
$$

$$
\frac{3}{12} + \frac{2}{12} = \frac{5}{12}
$$

---

## What to remember

1. **Bottoms must match.** If they do not, convert first.
2. **Find the smallest shared bottom** you can.
3. **Each fraction gets its own multiplier**, from its own bottom.
4. **Only add or subtract the tops.**
5. **Simplify the answer** before writing it down.
6. **Never add or subtract the bottoms.**
